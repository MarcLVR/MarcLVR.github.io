Federated learning: training a model without collecting the data

The data stays where it lives. The model travels to it. Here's how that works, where it breaks, and when it's worth the effort.

Objectives and algorithms, convergence under heterogeneity, secure aggregation, differential privacy, and the attacks that make both necessary.

Data. It never leaves the client. Model and updates. They travel.

Why not just collect the data?

Most machine learning starts by copying all the data into one place. Often that's not allowed, not practical or simply not a good idea.

Medical records, financial transactions and personal messages often can't legally leave the organisation or device that holds them. Billions of devices produce data all the time, and uploading all of it would be slow and expensive. And every central copy of sensitive data is one more thing that can be breached.

Centralised empirical risk minimisation assumes a single dataset \(D=\bigcup_k D_k\) that can be accessed by the optimiser. Federated learning (FL) keeps each \(D_k\) resident on client \(k\) and restricts the protocol to exchanging functions of the data, typically model parameters or their differences. The motivation is threefold: legal or contractual constraints on data movement (data minimisation, purpose limitation, data residency), the bandwidth and energy cost of moving raw data at edge scale, and the reduction of the attack surface that a single central store creates. FL is a system-level design choice, not a privacy guarantee in itself; formal guarantees require the mechanisms discussed later.

How one round works

The best-known method is called Federated Averaging, or FedAvg, introduced by Google researchers in 2016. Training happens in rounds. Click through the steps to see one.

After many rounds, the shared model has learned from everyone's data, even though the server never saw any of it. The first large-scale use was next-word prediction on phone keyboards: the model learned from what people typed without their messages leaving the phone.

Objective

With \(K\) clients holding \(n_k\) samples each and \(n=\sum_k n_k\), FL solves

\[\min_{w\in\mathbb{R}^d} F(w)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_k F_k(w),\qquad p_k=\frac{n_k}{n},\] \[F_k(w)=\frac{1}{n_k}\sum_{i\in D_k}\ell(w;x_i,y_i).\]

FedAvg generalises FedSGD (\(\tau=1\), full local batch), trading communication for local computation: with \(\tau\gg1\) the number of rounds to a target accuracy drops by one to two orders of magnitude in the original experiments [1]. The server step can itself be an optimiser: treating \(-\bar\Delta^t\) as a pseudo-gradient gives FedAdam, FedYogi and related methods [8].

FedAvg(K, C, E, B, η, T) initialise w⁰ for t = 0 … T−1: S_t ← random subset of ⌈C·K⌉ clients for k ∈ S_t in parallel: w ← wᵗ for each of E epochs, for each batch b ⊂ D_k of size B: w ← w − η ∇ℓ(w; b) Δ_k ← w − wᵗ wᵗ⁺¹ ← wᵗ + Σ_{k∈S_t} (n_k / Σ_{j∈S_t} n_j) · Δ_k

For convex, smooth \(F_k\), FedAvg with partial participation and decaying step size converges at \(\mathcal{O}(1/T)\) on non-IID data, but the constant grows with \(\tau\) and with client heterogeneity, and a fixed step size leaves a non-vanishing bias [7].

Two very different settings

"Federated learning" covers two situations that behave very differently in practice.

The regime determines the threat model, the failure modes and which algorithms are viable [2].

Cross-device

Millions of phones, watches or cars. Each holds a little data, is often offline, and joins only a few rounds.

\(K\sim10^6\)–\(10^{10}\), each client stateless and available with low probability per round; participation is sampled, dropouts are the norm, and per-client state (as in SCAFFOLD) is impractical. Secure aggregation and user-level DP are standard.

How the data is split

There's a second distinction. In horizontal federated learning, participants have the same kind of information about different people. In vertical federated learning, they have different information about the same people.

Horizontal FL partitions the sample space: \(\mathcal{X}_A=\mathcal{X}_B\), \(I_A\neq I_B\). Vertical FL partitions the feature space: \(I_A\cap I_B\neq\emptyset\), \(\mathcal{X}_A\neq\mathcal{X}_B\), so each sample's feature vector is \(x=[x^A;x^B]\) and labels usually sit with one party.

Vertical FL mechanics. Parties first align entities with private set intersection (PSI), so neither learns the other's non-overlapping IDs. For a generalised linear model the logit decomposes as \(z_i=w_A^\top x_i^A+w_B^\top x_i^B\); parties exchange partial logits and residuals under additively homomorphic encryption (e.g. Paillier) so the label holder can compute \(\partial\ell/\partial z_i\) without seeing \(x^A\). For deep models the common pattern is split learning: per-party bottom networks produce embeddings \(h^A,h^B\), and a top network owned by the label holder is trained on \([h^A;h^B]\). Intermediate activations and their gradients leak both features and labels, so vertical FL needs its own leakage analysis.

What makes it hard

Federated learning isn't just normal training spread over several machines. Four problems are specific to it.

Four properties separate FL from datacenter distributed training [2, 11]: statistical heterogeneity, communication cost, systems heterogeneity and the loss of data observability.

Every client's data is different

Each client reflects its own users and habits, so they pull the model in different directions. The result can be slower training, or a model that works for big clients and poorly for small ones.

Non-IID data: \(P_k(x,y)\neq P_j(x,y)\) through label skew, feature skew or concept shift. With \(\tau>1\) local steps, client optima \(w_k^\star\neq w^\star\) cause client drift (see below).

Communication is the bottleneck

Sending the model back and forth often costs more than the training itself, especially for big models on mobile networks.

Per-round uplink is \(\mathcal{O}(|S_t|\,d)\) and uplink bandwidth is asymmetric. Mitigations: more local work, quantisation, top-\(k\) sparsification with error feedback, low-rank or sketched updates, and parameter-efficient fine-tuning for large models.

Clients come and go

Devices disconnect, run slowly or have different hardware. The system has to make progress with whoever shows up.

Stragglers and dropouts bias the aggregate toward fast, well-connected clients. Variable \(\tau_k\) causes objective inconsistency, which FedNova corrects by normalising updates by local step count [10].

Nobody can look at the data

Cleaning data, checking labels, spotting bias and debugging all get harder when no one can inspect the training set.

Data validation, label-noise estimation, fairness auditing and error analysis must run as federated computations (federated analytics), themselves subject to the same privacy constraints.

Client drift and its corrections

Under the bounded gradient dissimilarity assumption \(\frac1K\sum_k\|\nabla F_k(w)\|^2\le G^2+B^2\|\nabla F(w)\|^2\), FedAvg's error has a term growing with \(\eta^2\tau^2G^2\). Three families of fixes:

Proximal regularisation. FedProx [9] has each client minimise \(F_k(w)+\frac{\mu}{2}\|w-w^t\|^2\), limiting how far local iterates travel.

Control variates. SCAFFOLD [5] keeps a server variate \(c\) and client variates \(c_k\) estimating the gradient gap, and corrects each local step: \(w\leftarrow w-\eta\,(g_k(w)-c_k+c)\). This removes the dependence on \(G\) but needs stateful clients.

Personalisation. When a single \(w^\star\) is the wrong target, optimise per-client models coupled to a global one, e.g. Ditto's \(\min_{v_k}F_k(v_k)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|v_k-w^\star\|^2\), local fine-tuning, or meta-learning initialisations.

Is it private?

Keeping the data at home is not the same as keeping it private.

The updates clients send are calculated from their data, so they carry information about it.

Updates are deterministic functions of \(D_k\); without further mechanisms FL offers data locality, not a privacy guarantee.

Researchers have shown that in some conditions, a server can partly or fully rebuild training examples from the updates it receives. Updates can also reveal whether a particular person's data was used. And because nobody sees the data, a malicious client can send a doctored update to damage the model or hide a backdoor in it.

Why updates leak

For a fully connected layer \(z=Wx+b\), the gradients are \(\nabla_W\ell=\delta x^\top\) and \(\nabla_b\ell=\delta\) with \(\delta=\partial\ell/\partial z\). For a single example, any row with \(\delta_i\neq0\) gives the input exactly:

\[x^\top=\frac{(\nabla_W\ell)_{i,:}}{(\nabla_b\ell)_i}.\]

For deeper networks and batches, gradient inversion solves \(\min_{x',y'}\;d\big(\nabla_w\ell(w;x',y'),\,\nabla_w\ell(w;x,y)\big)+\alpha\,\mathcal{R}(x')\), with \(d\) the Euclidean distance (DLG [12]) or cosine distance plus a total-variation prior (Geiping et al. [13]); the latter recovers recognisable images from multi-image batches and from FedAvg updates with several local steps. For cross-entropy the label of a single example follows from the sign pattern of the last-layer gradient (iDLG [14]). White-box membership inference against FL participants is also effective [15]. On the integrity side, model replacement attacks scale a backdoored update by roughly \(K/\eta\) so it survives averaging [16].

The three standard defences

Secure aggregation

A cryptographic protocol that lets the server see only the total of all updates, never any single one.

Protects individual updates. Doesn't hide what the total reveals.

Differential privacy

Caps how much any one person or participant can change the model, by trimming each update and adding carefully measured noise. It gives a provable privacy guarantee.

DP-FedAvg [4] provides user-level \((\varepsilon,\delta)\)-DP, where neighbouring datasets differ in one client's entire data: clip \(\bar\Delta_k=\Delta_k\min(1,S/\|\Delta_k\|_2)\), then release \(\frac{1}{qK}\big(\sum_{k\in S_t}\bar\Delta_k+\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2S^2I)\big)\). Privacy loss over \(T\) rounds with sampling rate \(q\) is tracked with the moments or Rényi accountant [17]; roughly \(\varepsilon=\mathcal{O}\big(q\sqrt{T\log(1/\delta)}/\sigma\big)\). Central DP with secure aggregation needs distributed noise generation; local DP avoids trusting the server at a much higher utility cost.

Protects against what the model and totals reveal. Costs accuracy.

Robust aggregation

Replaces the simple average with methods that ignore extreme updates, making it harder for one bad participant to poison the model.

Secure aggregation, in three numbers

Each pair of clients agrees on a random mask. One adds it, the other subtracts it. Individually the numbers the server receives look random. Added up, the masks cancel and only the true total remains.

The honest summary: federated learning is a strong foundation for privacy, not a guarantee of it. It moves the risk from a central database to the model updates, and those updates need their own protection.

Should you use it?

Federated learning adds real complexity. Answer four questions to see whether it's likely to pay off.

A quick screen before committing to the engineering and governance overhead.

  • Is moving the data to one place blocked, or too risky?
  • Is the data spread across several owners or many devices?
  • Would a shared model beat what each one can train alone?
  • Can the team work without inspecting the raw data?

Getting started

Open-source frameworks handle the coordination for you, and all of them let you simulate a federation on one laptop before deploying anything real.

Federated learning won't replace centralised training. It widens what's possible: models can now learn from data that used to be completely out of reach.

Open problems

Tight privacy–utility trade-offs for large models under user-level DP; compatibility of Byzantine robustness with secure aggregation; fairness across clients (e.g. q-FFL) versus average accuracy; federated unlearning with verifiable guarantees; incentive design and contribution valuation in cross-silo settings; and federated fine-tuning of foundation models under tight communication budgets [2].
