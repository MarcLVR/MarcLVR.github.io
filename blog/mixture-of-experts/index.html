Mixture of Experts: huge models that only use a fraction of themselves

Many of today's largest language models aren't one big network. They're many smaller ones, and each word only visits a few of them. Here's how that works, why it's cheaper, and what it changes.

Sparse conditional computation in transformers: gating functions, load balancing, capacity, expert parallelism, inference economics and what total-versus-active parameters means for evaluation and regulation.

Token. One piece of text. Active expert. Runs for this token. Idle expert. Stored, but not used.

Why not just make the model bigger?

Bigger models are usually better. But in a normal model, every part of the network does work for every single word. Double the size and you double the cost of every answer.

A Mixture of Experts (MoE) model breaks that link. It holds a lot of knowledge in many separate blocks, called experts, but for each word it only switches on the few that are most relevant. You get the capacity of a very large model at roughly the running cost of a much smaller one.

In a dense transformer, forward FLOPs per token scale as \(\approx 2N\) for \(N\) parameters, so capacity and per-token compute are locked together. Conditional computation decouples them: a sparsely activated layer holds \(N_{\text{total}}\) parameters but evaluates only \(N_{\text{active}}\ll N_{\text{total}}\) per token, chosen by a learned, input-dependent gate [1, 2]. Empirically, at fixed training compute, sparse models reach a given loss with fewer FLOPs than dense ones, and the gap grows with expert count up to a point [4, 5]. The price is paid in memory, communication and optimisation stability, not in arithmetic.

What happens inside one layer

A language model is a stack of layers. In an MoE model, part of each layer, the part that does most of the "thinking" about each word, is replaced by a set of experts plus a small router that decides which experts to use. Click through the steps.

In a transformer block, the MoE sublayer replaces the position-wise feed-forward network (FFN), in every block or every other block; attention stays dense. Each expert \(E_i\) is an ordinary FFN (typically SwiGLU). Step through the routing of one token; the formal description follows.

This happens independently for every token in every MoE layer, so a single sentence takes many different paths through the model. Nobody tells the router which expert to use: it learns that during training, together with the experts themselves.

Gating

Shazeer et al. [2] introduced the noisy top-\(k\) gate for LSTMs, the form most later work builds on:

\[G(x)=\mathrm{softmax}\big(\mathrm{KeepTopK}(H(x),k)\big),\] \[H(x)_i=(W_r x)_i+\varepsilon_i\cdot\mathrm{softplus}\big((W_{\text{noise}}x)_i\big),\]

with \(\varepsilon_i\sim\mathcal N(0,1)\) and KeepTopK setting non-selected logits to \(-\infty\). The layer output is \(y=\sum_{i=1}^{N}G(x)_i\,E_i(x)\), and since \(G(x)\) is \(k\)-sparse only \(k\) experts are computed. The router receives gradient only through the selected gate values, so the selection itself is non-differentiable; it is learned indirectly through which experts' weights help the loss. Switch Transformer [4] showed \(k=1\) works and simplifies routing; GShard [3] and Mixtral [6] use \(k=2\); DeepSeek-V3 [8] selects 8 of 256 fine-grained experts with sigmoid affinities.

MoE_layer(X ∈ ℝ^{T×d}, W_r, {E_1..E_N}, k) P ← softmax(X W_rᵀ) # T × N router probs for each token t: S_t ← indices of top-k entries of P[t] g_t ← P[t, S_t] / Σ P[t, S_t] # renormalise for each expert i in parallel: B_i ← { t : i ∈ S_t } # dispatch (all-to-all) Y_i ← E_i(X[B_i]) for each token t: y_t ← Σ_{i∈S_t} g_t[i] · Y_i[t] # combine (all-to-all) return Y

Fine-grained and shared experts

DeepSeekMoE [7] splits each expert into \(m\) smaller ones and activates \(mk\) of them, keeping compute constant while increasing the number of possible combinations from \(\binom{N}{k}\) to \(\binom{mN}{mk}\). It also adds \(K_s\) shared experts that every token passes through, so common knowledge isn't duplicated across routed experts:

\[h=x+\sum_{i=1}^{K_s}E^{\text{sh}}_i(x)+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{T}}g_i\,E_i(x).\]

One model, two sizes

This is the key idea to remember. An MoE model has a total size, everything it stores, and an active size, the part that actually runs for each word. Try changing the number of experts and how many each word uses.

With \(N_s\) always-on parameters (embeddings, attention, router, shared experts) and per-expert parameters \(N_e\) summed over MoE layers, \(N_{\text{total}}=N_s+N\cdot N_e\) and \(N_{\text{active}}=N_s+k\cdot N_e\). Memory follows the first; per-token FLOPs follow the second.

Are the experts really experts?

Not in the way the name suggests.

There's no "maths expert" or "law expert". Experts emerge from training, and what they specialise in is rarely something a person would name.

Expert assignment is an emergent property of joint optimisation, with no supervision on semantics.

When Mistral AI studied which experts their Mixtral model picked, they found little difference between topics: maths papers, biology articles and philosophy texts used the experts in similar proportions. What they did find was a pattern by form: in Python code, for example, indentation and the word self tended to go to the same expert, and neighbouring words often took the same route [6].

That's worth knowing because "which expert handled this?" sounds like an explanation of the model's answer. It isn't.

Jiang et al. [6] measured expert selection across subsets of The Pile (ArXiv, PubMed, PhilPapers, GitHub, …) at layers 0, 15 and 31 of Mixtral and found no clear domain specialisation, except a mildly different distribution for DM Mathematics, likely due to its synthetic nature. Routing was instead correlated with syntax and position: consecutive tokens were assigned the same expert far more often than chance in deeper layers, and code tokens such as indentation and self concentrated on particular experts. Fine-grained and shared-expert designs [7] are motivated precisely by the claim that coarse experts end up redundant; whether this yields more interpretable specialisation is still an open question, and routing traces should not be treated as attributions.

What makes it hard

MoE saves computation, but it isn't free. Four problems come with it.

The arithmetic savings are offset by four engineering problems: load imbalance, memory footprint, communication, and optimisation instability.

Some experts get all the work

Left alone, the router tends to favour a few experts. Those get better, so they get picked even more, while the others are wasted. Training has to actively push for balance.

Routing collapse: a positive feedback loop between router preference and expert quality. Mitigated with auxiliary balance losses, bias-based balancing, or balanced assignment by construction (see below).

Cheap to run, not cheap to hold

Each word only uses a few experts, but the next word might need any of the others. So the whole model still has to sit in memory, and memory is often the most expensive part of running it.

Weights for all \(N\) experts must be resident (or paged in at high cost). Per-token FLOPs scale with \(N_{\text{active}}\), but HBM capacity, and hence GPU count, scales with \(N_{\text{total}}\).

Words have to travel between machines

Big MoE models are spread across many chips, with different experts on different chips. Every layer, each word has to be sent to wherever its experts live and back again.

Expert parallelism shards experts across devices; each MoE layer needs two all-to-all collectives (dispatch and combine) whose cost grows with tokens × \(k\) × \(d\) and with interconnect topology.

Training is touchier

MoE models have historically been less stable to train and easier to over-fit when adapted with small amounts of data. That has improved a lot, but it still needs care.

Router logits can grow and cause loss spikes, especially in low precision; fine-tuning on small datasets overfits more readily than dense models of matched quality [5], though instruction tuning helps MoE disproportionately [13].

Balancing, capacity and stability

Auxiliary loss. Switch Transformer [4] adds, per MoE layer over a batch of \(T\) tokens,

\[\mathcal{L}_{\text{aux}}=\alpha\,N\sum_{i=1}^{N}f_i\,P_i,\] \[f_i=\frac1T\sum_{t}\mathbb{1}[\arg\max p_t=i],\qquad P_i=\frac1T\sum_t p_{t,i},\]

which is minimised under uniform routing; \(f\) is non-differentiable, so the gradient flows through \(P\). Typical \(\alpha=10^{-2}\). Too large an \(\alpha\) trades model quality for balance.

Capacity. For static shapes each expert processes at most \(C=\mathrm{cf}\cdot kT/N\) tokens; overflow tokens are dropped and carried by the residual stream. MegaBlocks [11] avoids dropping with block-sparse kernels that handle variable expert batch sizes.

Auxiliary-loss-free balancing. DeepSeek-V3 [8, 9] adds a per-expert bias \(b_i\) to the affinity used only for selection (not for the gate value), and after each step nudges \(b_i\leftarrow b_i-\gamma\,\mathrm{sign}(f_i-\bar f)\). This balances load without a gradient term competing with the language-modelling loss; a very small sequence-level auxiliary loss is kept as a safeguard.

Router z-loss. ST-MoE [5] penalises large router logits, \(\mathcal{L}_z=\frac1T\sum_t\big(\log\sum_i e^{z_{t,i}}\big)^2\), which removes most training instabilities from round-off in the router softmax.

Balanced by construction. Expert-choice routing [10] lets each expert pick its top-\(C\) tokens, giving perfect balance and a variable number of experts per token, but it peeks at other tokens in the batch, which complicates autoregressive decoding and creates a cross-request side channel [17]. BASE layers [12] solve a linear assignment problem; hash layers route by a fixed hash of the token id. Soft MoE [14] replaces discrete assignment with learned weighted mixtures of tokens, fully differentiable but less natural for causal decoders.

Inference economics

Decoding at small batch size is memory-bandwidth bound: latency tracks bytes read per step, which tracks \(N_{\text{active}}\), so an MoE decodes much faster than a dense model of the same total size. At batch size \(B\), assuming roughly uniform independent routing, the expected number of distinct experts touched per layer is

\[\mathbb{E}[\#\text{experts}]=N\Big(1-\big(1-\tfrac{k}{N}\big)^{B}\Big),\]

which approaches \(N\) quickly (with \(N=8,k=2\), already \(\approx 7.2\) at \(B=8\); see the figure above). So at high throughput the weight-loading advantage fades and the win is in FLOPs, while memory remains set by \(N_{\text{total}}\). This is why MoE favours large, batched, expert-parallel deployments and is less attractive on a single memory-constrained device, where quantisation or expert offloading is required.

Why it matters beyond the lab

If you procure, deploy or govern AI rather than build it, MoE still changes a few things you should ask about.

For model risk, procurement and compliance, the total/active split changes how model scale, cost and transparency claims should be read.

"How big is it?" has two answers

Ask for both total and active size. A model described as "47 billion parameters" may run like a 13-billion one but need the hardware of a 47-billion one. Comparing an MoE with a normal model on one number is misleading.

Benchmarks normalised by parameter count should state which count. Quality tracks a mix of both, per-token cost tracks \(N_{\text{active}}\), and serving footprint tracks \(N_{\text{total}}\) at the chosen precision.

Affects: model comparison, vendor claims, cost estimates.

Regulation counts compute, not parameters

The EU AI Act treats general-purpose AI models as potentially carrying systemic risk based on the amount of computation used to train them, not on how many parameters they have. Because MoE models use less computation per word, they can be much larger in parameters for the same training budget.

Article 51 of the AI Act presumes systemic risk for GPAI models with cumulative training compute above \(10^{25}\) FLOPs [15]. With \(C\approx6\,N_{\text{active}}\,D\) for \(D\) training tokens, an MoE can carry several times the parameters of a dense model at the same \(C\). Parameter count is a poor proxy for regulatory scale; ask providers for the compute estimate.

Affects: GPAI classification, technical documentation requests.

Self-hosting needs room for every expert

When data has to stay in-house or in the EU, teams often run open models on their own hardware. With MoE, the hardware bill is set by the total size, even though most of it sits idle for any given word.

On-premise or sovereign-cloud deployments for data residency must size GPU memory for \(N_{\text{total}}\) plus KV cache. Quantisation (8- or 4-bit) and expert offloading trade latency or quality for footprint, and both should be re-evaluated on the task, not assumed neutral.

Affects: GDPR data residency, infrastructure budget.

Routing is visible, but it isn't an explanation

You can log which experts handled each word. That's useful for monitoring and debugging, but since experts aren't topic specialists, it doesn't tell you why the model said what it said.

Router traces are cheap, discrete telemetry, useful for drift and load monitoring. They are not feature attributions; treat them like any other internal activation when building explainability evidence.

Affects: explainability claims, monitoring design.

MoE and privacy

MoE isn't a privacy technique. But it brings two privacy issues that normal models don't have, and it fits naturally with federated learning.

Sparse routing introduces dependencies between requests and a new class of input-derived data. It is also a natural way to parameterise personalised federated learning.

Who shares your batch can matter

Servers process many people's requests together, and each expert can only take so many words at once. With some routing designs, whether your words get processed depends on what others in the same batch sent. Researchers showed that an attacker whose requests land in the same batch as yours can use this to rebuild your prompt [17]. It's a proof of concept under narrow conditions, not an attack on today's chatbots. Still, normal models don't have this weakness at all.

Yona et al. [17] show that under Expert-Choice routing with capacity limits, an adversary sharing a batch with a victim can recover the victim's prompt by observing which of its own tokens are dropped. The demonstration used a two-layer Mixtral variant and relied on the tie-breaking of torch.topk, with \(\mathcal{O}(VM^2)\) queries, about 100 per token in their setup. Mitigations include per-request capacity isolation, token-choice routing with dropless kernels [11] and randomised tie-breaking. In multi-tenant serving, treat batch composition as a confidentiality boundary.

Affects: shared hosting, vendor due diligence.

Routing logs are personal data too

The record of which experts handled each word is calculated from what the user typed. If you keep it for monitoring, under GDPR treat it like the prompts themselves: a clear purpose, limited retention and controlled access.

Router traces are a deterministic function of the input. Linked to request or user identifiers they are pseudonymous data in GDPR scope (Recital 26), not anonymous telemetry. Aggregate to per-expert load statistics before retention where monitoring allows.

Affects: logging design, records of processing, retention policy.

A natural fit for federated learning

In federated learning, every participant's data is different, so one shared model fits nobody perfectly. A mixture of experts helps. Each participant combines a shared expert, trained together, with a private one that never leaves their side, and a router learns how much to trust each.

Peterson et al. [18] gate between a differentially private federated general model and a per-user private domain expert, with the largest gains when DP is applied to the shared model. FedMix [19] trains an ensemble and selects a user-specific subset of members. Clients that train only the experts they use also cut uplink and on-device memory. Routing statistics reveal a client's data distribution, so they need the same protection as model updates (see federated learning).

Affects: personalisation, communication cost.

Check yourself

Four quick questions. Pick an answer to see why.

  1. Mixtral 8x7B has about how many parameters in total?

    46.7 billion. Only the feed-forward part is copied eight times; attention and embeddings are shared, so it isn't 8 × 7. And 12.9 billion is the active size, the part used for each word.

  2. What mostly decides how much hardware you need to run an MoE model?

    Its total size. Any expert might be needed for the next word, so all of them must sit in memory. The active size sets the cost and speed of each word, not the memory.

  3. A user asks a chemistry question. Where does the router send it?

    Word by word, not by topic. Routing happens per word and per layer, and studies of Mixtral found little topic specialisation. Experts are not human-style specialists.

  4. Under the EU AI Act, what triggers the presumption that a general-purpose model carries systemic risk?

    Training compute, above 1025 operations (Article 51). MoE models use less computation per word, so they can have far more parameters than a normal model for the same training budget.

The short version

Mixture of Experts is the reason some of the largest open models can be run at all. It trades memory and engineering complexity for a large saving in computation per word. It doesn't make models smarter by itself, and its experts aren't specialists in any human sense, but it changes what "model size" means. Ask for two numbers, not one.

MoE decouples capacity from per-token compute at the cost of memory, communication and routing complexity. It is now the default architecture for frontier-scale open-weight LLMs. For evaluation and governance, report \(N_{\text{total}}\), \(N_{\text{active}}\) and training compute separately.

Open problems

Interpretability of expert specialisation; routing robustness under distribution shift; efficient fine-tuning and continual learning without router drift; expert pruning and merging for deployment; upcycling dense checkpoints into MoE [16]; and scaling laws that account jointly for total parameters, active parameters and granularity.
